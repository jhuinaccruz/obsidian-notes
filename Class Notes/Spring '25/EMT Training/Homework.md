### Diseases
__Hepatitis__: A viral infection of the liver that can cause severe damage (cirrhosis) and cancer
- Hepatitis B and hepatitis C are most worrisome to health care providers.
- According to the CDC, an estimated 862,000 people in the United States have chronic hepatitis B infection.
- A vaccine for hepatitis B is available and recommended for all health care providers.
- People who are exposed to hepatitis B may begin to experience signs and symptoms within 2 weeks to 6 months.

The signs and symptoms of hepatitis B include:
- Fatigue
- Nausea
- Loss of appetite
- Abdominal pain
- Jaundice (yellowing of the skin)
- Asymptomatic people can infect others who are exposed to their blood.

__HIV__: attacks white blood cells and destroys the body’s ability to fight infection.

__Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) is__: the most advanced stage of HIV infection

__When HIV infection progresses to AIDS, the disease__: is nearly always fatal.

__Tuberculosis (TB)__: A serious disease that affects the respiratory system caused by bacteria that settle in the lungs.

[Cardiovascular disease (CVD)](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary"): A spectrum of disease processes affecting the heart and circulatory system.
- The death rate from CVD in the United States is 219.4 per 100,000 people.
- Almost half of these deaths are due to fatal heart attacks attributed to [coronary artery disease (CAD)](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
- CAD causes approximately 360,000 cases of [sudden cardiac arrest (SCA)](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") in an out-of-hospital setting.
- SCA is a condition in which the heart suddenly and unexpectedly stops beating. Death usually occurs if SCA is not treated within minutes.

Risk factors for CVD fall into two categories:
 - Modifiable risk factors are behaviors or conditions that can be controlled, such as:
        - Cigarette smoking
        - High blood pressure
        - High cholesterol
        - Lack of exercise
        - Stress
        - Obesity
 - Nonmodifiable risk factors are those that cannot be controlled, such as:
        - Sex
        - Age
        - Heredity
- Cigarette smokers are much more likely to develop CVD, suffer a heart attack, or have a stroke compared to nonsmokers.
- High blood pressure significantly increases the chances of CAD and is a major risk factor for a stroke.
- High cholesterol can result from a diet high in saturated fats. A fatty diet has been associated with the development of fatty deposits on the artery walls ([atherosclerosis](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")).
- Obesity is also a high-risk factor because excessive weight strains the heart.

The number of deaths from SCA can be reduced if the [chain of survival](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") takes place.
- Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) chain of survival describes the importance of:
    - Early activation of the emergency response system
    - Care initiated by the bystander
    - Care provided by EMS personnel until the patient arrives at the hospital
    - Recovery of the cardiac arrest survivor
- The In-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) chain of survival describes the sequence of care that:
    - Begins with early recognition and prevention of cardiac arrest
    - Includes an integrated system of care provided by physicians, nurses, and respiratory therapists if cardiac arrest occurs
    - Supports the recovery of the cardiac arrest survivor

#### Respiratory System
>*The respiratory system delivers oxygen to the lungs and removes waste products such as carbon dioxide.*
>*If the respiratory system stops for any reason, the body will die within minutes.*


>*During breathing, air enters the mouth and nose and passes through the pharynx and past the epiglottis, a flap of cartilage that covers the glottis during swallowing, preventing food and liquids from entering the lungs.*
>*Air enters the trachea, which branches into bronchi that enter each lung. Each bronchial tube divides into bronchioles, ending in alveoli, which are enclosed within capillaries.*
	*This is where oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged (pulmonary respiration).*

>*Air enters and is expelled from the lungs through the actions of the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm.*
>*During inhalation, the diaphragm contracts, moving downward and flattening, and the intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs upward and outward.*
>*As the chest expands, the pressure inside the chest cavity becomes less than the pressure outside the body.*
>*This vacuum pulls air in and expands the lungs to equalize the pressure.
>When the diaphragm and intercostal muscles relax, they force the air out (exhalation) in the same amount as was initially inhaled.*
>*Adults normally breathe 12 to 20 breaths per minute (respiratory rate).
>Normal breathing rates for children are 12 to 40 breaths per minute, depending on age.*
>*Specialized areas of the brain sense the levels of oxygen and carbon dioxide in the blood and regulate the breathing rate and depth to maintain an appropriate balance.*

>*The cardiovascular system is composed of the heart and blood vessels.*
>*It functions to deliver oxygen and nutrients throughout the body and to remove carbon dioxide and other metabolic waste.*


>*The heart lies behind and slightly to the left of the lower sternum, between the lungs, and in front of the esophagus and the trachea.*

>*The myocardium is a double pump with four chambers: the right and left atria and the right and left ventricles.*


>*The left and right sides of the heart are separated by a wall called the septum.*


>*The right side of the heart receives oxygen-poor venous blood from the body through the vena cavae.*
>*Blood enters the right atrium and is pumped to the right ventricle and then to the lungs, where carbon dioxide in the blood is exchanged for oxygen.*
>*The oxygen-rich blood returns to the left atrium of the heart, which pumps it to the left ventricle and then through the body.*

#### Nervous System
>*The nervous system allows the body to react to internal and external stimulation and controls movement.
>Signals travel from the brain through a network of nerves that extends down the spinal cord and branches out through the body.
>These nerves send and receive messages at an incredible rate of speed.*

>*The brain and spinal cord serve as the headquarters and communications system for the body.
>The brain is encased in the skull, and the spinal cord is inside the vertebrae of the spine.
>The spinal cord relays sensory information from peripheral nerves to the brain and forwards signals from the brain to the body through motor fibers.
>Injury to any area of the spinal cord can disrupt the flow of information.*

>*The brain has three main parts:
>- The cerebrum controls thought, sensation, memory, and voluntary motions such as walking or picking up an object.
>- The cerebellum controls balance by coordinating signals from the eyes and inner ears.
>- The medulla oblongata connects the cerebrum and the cerebellum to the spinal cord.
>- It controls involuntary motions, such as breathing, heart rate, and digestion.*

>*When either the respiratory or the cardiovascular system fails, cells in the brain begin to die in as little as 4 to 6 minutes because of the lack of oxygen.
>Rescue breathing and cardiopulmonary resuscitation can help keep the brain oxygenated until the patient can receive specialized medical care.*

#### Respiratory System Failure

>*The signs and symptoms of respiratory distress include the following:
>- Signs of hypoxia (low oxygen levels in the blood), which may include restlessness, anxiety, and confusion
>- Signs of airway obstruction or swelling, which may include grunting, stridor, and an inability to speak
>- Flared nostrils
>- Unusually rapid, deep, or irregular breathing
>- Straining to breathe (use of facial and neck muscles, retraction of muscles between the ribs, tracheal tugging)
>- Cyanosis of fingernails and around lips

>*The most severe sign of respiratory system failure is respiratory arrest. In this condition, breathing stops and the skin becomes cyanotic or ashen.
>Without rapid intervention, brain cells will die from oxygen deprivation.*

#### Cardiovascular System Failure

>	This is referred to as coronary artery disease (CAD).

>Angina is a sign of CAD.

#### Acute Myocardial Infarction
- Heart disease can narrow the [coronary arteries](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
    - This is referred to as coronary artery disease (CAD).
- When the inside of one of these arteries narrows so much that blood flow is restricted, oxygen delivery to the heart diminishes, impairing heart function.
- A mild form of diminished blood flow can result in [angina](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary"), where the heart is temporarily deprived of oxygen ([ischemia](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")) due to partial narrowing of a coronary artery.
    - Angina is a sign of CAD.
- Stable angina occurs after a predictable amount of physical exertion.
    - It usually lasts less than 15 minutes and is relieved by rest and/or nitroglycerin tablets or spray.
- Unstable angina occurs during periods of nonexertion and often is not relieved by rest and/or nitroglycerin.
    - [Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") is a spectrum of clinical disease that refers to unstable angina and acute myocardial infarction (heart attack). The most common symptom of ACS is chest pain, pressure, or discomfort.

#### Stroke
- A [stroke](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") occurs when:
    - A blood vessel in the brain delivering oxygen-rich blood becomes occluded (ischemic stroke)
        - Approximately 87% of strokes are ischemic.
    - A blood vessel ruptures (hemorrhagic stroke)
- The signs and symptoms of a stroke include:
    - Weakness, numbness, or paralysis of the face, arm, or leg on one side of the body
    - Blurred or decreased vision, especially in one eye
    - Difficulty speaking or understanding speech
    - Dizziness or loss of balance
    - Sudden, severe, and unexplained headache
- Use stroke assessment tools, such as the Cincinnati Stroke Scale or the National Institutes of Health (NIH) stroke scale, that check for physical findings like:
    - Facial droop
    - Arm weakness/drift
    - Speech abnormalities
- Evaluate people for other causes of altered mental status, including seizures and hypoglycemia.
- If the onset of the stroke symptoms is within the past 3 hours (4 ½ hours in certain circumstances), the patient may be eligible to receive a fibrinolytic medication that can dissolve the clot in the affected cerebral artery.
### Patient Assessment
#### Assessing the Emergency Scene
- Your first task at an emergency scene is to evaluate for hazards that could threaten safety.
- If the scene is unsafe, call for the appropriate resources (e.g., hospital security, fire department, law enforcement).
- Be aware of actual and potential threats to safety.
- Scene assessment can also reveal possible causes of the patient’s condition.
- If you are the person on scene responsible for patient care, you must quickly gain control. Introduce yourself to other health care providers and bystanders in a calm, confident, and professional manner.
#### Assessing the Patient
- Take standard precautions against disease transmission and begin assessing the patient.
- The [primary assessment](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") allows you to quickly determine the most life-threatening condition the patient has.
- The three most important body systems in the primary assessment are:
    - The nervous system (Is the patient conscious?)
    - The respiratory system (Is the patient breathing?)
    - The cardiovascular system (Is the patient’s heart beating? Is there uncontrolled bleeding?)
- Once you have assessed the patient, determine whether you have adequate personnel and equipment to provide the necessary patient care.
#### Assessing Responsiveness
- Begin the primary assessment by checking for responsiveness.
- If the patient is lying motionless, tap or gently shake their shoulder, and shout, “Are you okay?”
- If the patient does not respond, they are [unresponsive](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
- Call for help and activate the appropriate response system.
    - If help comes, have them retrieve AED and proceed with your assessment.
    - If help does not come, quickly go to get an AED and then continue caring for the patient.
#### Assessing Breathing and Circulation
- If the patient is unresponsive, simultaneously check for signs of breathing and for a pulse.
- Check for breathing by looking at the patient’s chest to see if it is rising and falling.
- Check the pulse at the carotid artery by placing your fingers in the groove between the Adam’s apple and the neck muscle on the side of the patient’s neck nearest you.
    - Never try to feel the carotid pulse on both sides of the neck at the same time.
- You can also check for a pulse at the femoral artery.
- For infants (birth to 1 year old), check the brachial pulse, located on the inside of the upper arm.
>- If the patient is unresponsive; is breathing adequately; and is not suspected of having injury to the spine, hips, or pelvis, place the patient into the [recovery position](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").

##### Respiratory Arrest
- If the patient is not breathing or has abnormal breathing (i.e., only gasping) but has a _definite_ pulse, open the patient’s airway and perform [rescue breathing](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
    - For patients without a suspected spinal injury, use the [head tilt–chin lift maneuver](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
    - If you suspect a spinal injury, open the patient’s airway with the [jaw-thrust maneuver](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
- If you cannot adequately open the patient’s airway with the jaw-thrust maneuver, perform the head tilt–chin lift maneuver, moving the neck only as much as needed to open the airway.
##### Cardiopulmonary Arrest
- If the patient is not breathing or has abnormal breathing (i.e., only gasps) and does not have a _definite_ pulse:
    - Perform 30 chest compressions
    - Open the airway
    - Give 2 rescue breaths
- Continue chest compressions and rescue breaths until a defibrillator (manual or automated external) has been applied and is ready to analyze the patient’s cardiac rhythm.

### Basic Life Support for Adults and Children
#### Patient Assessment
- If the patient is conscious and breathing normally, assist them as needed.
- If the patient is unresponsive, quickly look at the patient’s chest to see if it is rising and falling at a regular rate and check for a pulse.
- These two checks should occur simultaneously and take no more than 10 seconds.
- If the patient is unresponsive but is breathing normally and has a pulse, position them in the recovery position.
- If the patient is unresponsive, is not breathing or is breathing abnormally, and does not have a pulse, begin CPR, starting with chest compressions.
##### Check for Responsiveness
- Roll the patient, keeping the head, neck, shoulders, back, and pelvis aligned.
- After the patient is properly positioned, tap them and shout, “Are you okay?”
- If the patient is unresponsive, call for help.
##### Check for Breathing and a Pulse
- If the patient is unresponsive, assess for breathing by looking to see if the chest is rising and falling normally while simultaneously checking for a pulse.
    - This should take no more than 10 seconds.
- In an adult or child, the pulse is checked by locating the carotid artery with your index and middle fingers.
- Locate the [thyroid cartilage](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") and slide your fingers toward you, into the groove at the side of the neck.
- Press down gently to feel for the carotid pulse.
    - Feel for a pulse for at least 5 seconds but no more than 10 seconds.
    - You may also check for a femoral pulse.
- If a pulse is present but the patient is not breathing or is breathing abnormally, provide rescue breathing.
- If a pulse is absent, perform CPR starting with chest compressions.
- Practice by feeling your own pulse.
##### Opening the Airway and Providing Rescue Breathing
- If an unresponsive patient is not breathing or is breathing abnormally but has a pulse, provide rescue breathing.
- When a person becomes unconscious, all of the body’s muscles relax, including the tongue, which can fall back into the throat (pharynx) and block the airway.
    - Moving the jaw forward moves the tongue away from the back of the throat.
- There are two common maneuvers for opening the airway:
    - The head tilt–chin lift maneuver
    - The jaw-thrust maneuver
- The head tilt–chin lift maneuver is used when no spinal injury is suspected.
    - Place one hand, palm down, on the patient’s forehead and tilt the head back.
    - Place two fingers of your other hand on the bony part of the patient’s chin and lift up.
        - In children, be sure not to hyperextend the neck, because this may cause obstruction of the airway.
- The jaw-thrust maneuver allows you to lift the patient’s jaw without tilting the head back or extending the neck.
- Place your index and middle fingers on the angles of the lower jaw and your thumbs on the cheekbones.
- Move the lower jaw forward without tilting the head back.
- If the jaw-thrust maneuver is unsuccessful, carefully perform the head tilt–chin lift maneuver—even if you suspect a spinal injury.
- Rescue breathing is the skill of blowing air into the lungs of a nonbreathing ([apneic](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")) patient.
- Deliver each rescue breath over a period of 1 second—just enough to produce visible chest rise.
- _Do not_ ventilate too fast or with too much volume, a condition known as [hyperventilation](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary").
    - Hyperventilating may produce gastric distention or reduce blood return to the heart.
- If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and attempt to deliver another breath.
- If two attempts at delivering rescue breathing are unsuccessful, suspect an airway obstruction that needs to be cleared.
- Rescue breathing can be performed with various barrier ventilation devices or more advanced ventilation devices.
#### Mouth-to-Mask Method
1. Position yourself at the patient's head
2. Open the patient's airway with the head tilt-chin lift or the jaw-thrust maneuver
3. Place the mask over the patient's mouth and nose
4. Using both hands, grasp the mask and the patient's jaw. Press down on the mask with your thumbs as you lift up on the jaw with your fingers. This will create a good seal between the mask and the face.
5. Breathe into the one-way valve. Each breath should occur over a period of one second - ensure that it is just enough to produce visible chest rise. Release pressure on the mask to allow air to escape.

#### Mask-to-Stoma Method
- A special situation involves a nonbreathing patient who has had a [laryngectomy](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") (surgical removal of the [larynx](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")).
- The patient breathes through a [stoma](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") in the front of the neck.
- If the patient is not breathing, place a small mask over the stoma and ventilate the patient.
- In some patients, there is still a connection between the upper airway and the stoma.
    - When you breathe into the stoma, the patient’s mouth and nose must be closed to prevent air from flowing into the upper airway.
#### Performing CPR
##### Adult
1. Position the patient so they are flat on their back on a hard surface. Position yourself so that your knees are alongside the patient's chest
2. Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, between the nipples. Place your other hand on top of the first. Lock your fingers together and pull them upwards so that the only thing touching the patient's chest is the heel of your hand
3. Lean forward so your shoulders are directly over your hands and the patient's sternum. Keep your arms straight and compress the sternum at least 2 inches (5 cm). Relax between compressions, allowing the chest to fully recoil; avoid leaning on the patient's chest between compressions. Give 30 compressions, counting each one out loud, at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute
4. After 30 chest compressions, open the airway and give 2 breaths (1 second each). Ensure that each breath produces visible chest rise
5. Continue the cycle of 30 chest compressions and 2 breaths. Do not stop unless the patient begins to move, you are replaced by someone of equal or higher training, or you are alone and become too fatigued to continue. When the AED arrives, continue compressions while it is turned on and applied. Do not stop until the voice prompts you to stop so it can analyze and shock if necessary.
##### Children
1. Position the patient so they are flat on their back on a hard surface. Position yourself so that your knees are alongside the patient's chest
2. Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest, between the nipples. For larger children, you should use two hands, as with an adult.
3. Lean forward so your shoulders are directly over your hands and the patient's sternum. Keep your arms straight and compress the sternum at least 2 inches (5 cm). Relax between compressions, allowing the chest to fully recoil; avoid leaning on the patient's chest between compressions. Give 30 compressions, counting each one out loud, at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute
4. After 30 chest compressions, open the airway and give 2 breaths (1 second each). Ensure that each breath produces visible chest rise
5. Continue the cycle of 30 chest compressions and 2 breaths. Do not stop unless the patient begins to move, you are replaced by someone of equal or higher training, or you are alone and become too fatigued to continue. When the AED arrives, continue compressions while it is turned on and applied. Do not stop until the voice prompts you to "not touch the patient"

##### Two-Person CPR
- Two-person CPR has several distinct advantages over one-person CPR:
    - Providers do not tire as quickly and resuscitation efforts can be more effective.
    - One provider can perform chest compressions while the other provider checks the effectiveness of the compressions by monitoring for a pulse.
        - You should be able to feel a carotid pulse during adequately performed chest compressions.
- In two-person CPR, one provider performs chest compressions and the second provider delivers rescue breaths.
    - If the patient is an adult, a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 30 to 2 should be used.
    - If the patient is a child, a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 15 to 2 should be used.
- Work on opposite sides of the patient so you can switch functions easily.
    - Switch functions every 2 minutes in order to minimize provider fatigue.
- When an advanced airway is in place during two-person adult or child CPR, you no longer need to deliver cycles of CPR.
- Ventilate the adult or child at a rate of 10 breaths per minute (1 breath every 6 seconds) and perform continuous chest compressions at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute.
- Do not attempt to synchronize breaths and compressions.

##### Stopping CPR
You can discontinue CPR in the following situations:
- Return of spontaneous circulation occurs (a pulse returns) or signs of life return.
- Another trained provider replaces you.
- A physician tells you to stop.
- You are too physically exhausted to continue.
- The scene becomes unsafe.

##### Common Mistakes
- Even when CPR is performed correctly, there are potential complications, including:
    - Fractures of the ribs or sternum
    - Separation of the rib cartilage
    - Bruising of the heart and lungs
    - Puncture of the lungs, liver, spleen, or heart from fractured ribs
    - Ruptured lungs (most often associated with excessive ventilation of the lungs in children and infants)
- Pay careful attention to your form.
- Some common mistakes people make when performing rescue breathing and CPR include:
    - Failing to adequately open the airway
    - Failing to maintain an open airway
    - Failing to maintain an adequate mask seal over the nose and mouth
    - Not providing adequate breaths or breathing too fast or too forcefully
    - Completing CPR cycles too slowly or too quickly
    - Not placing the patient on a hard or level surface for effective chest compressions
    - Performing chest compressions with the elbows bent instead of with straight arms
    - Performing chest compressions with the hands in the wrong location (often too low on the sternum)
    - Using the wrong compression rate
    - Performing chest compressions that are too shallow or too deep or with jerky movements
#### Airway Obstruction
- [Airway obstruction](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") (choking) is the fourth leading cause of unintentional injury death.
- Immediate recognition and removal of the obstruction is the key to preventing hypoxia, loss of consciousness, and cardiac arrest.
    - Food is the most common foreign body airway obstruction in a conscious adult.
    - Small objects are common causes of airway obstruction in children.
    - In any unresponsive patient, the tongue is the most common cause of airway obstruction.
- Swelling of the airway can result from:
    - A severe allergic reaction
    - Infections such as [croup](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") or [epiglottitis](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")
    - Trauma
- If you are unsure whether the obstruction is caused by a foreign body or by swelling, assume the obstruction is a foreign body.
- If you believe the cause of the obstruction is swelling, request advanced life support personnel immediately.
##### Types of Airway Obstruction
- With a [mild airway obstruction](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary"), the patient has adequate air exchange.
    - If the patient is coughing, encourage them to continue.
    - Do not interfere with the patient’s own attempts to expel the obstruction. Remain with the patient and be ready to intervene if their condition deteriorates.
- A [severe airway obstruction](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary") occurs when the airway is completely blocked.
    - The patient:
        - Will not be able to speak, cough, cry, or breathe
        - May display the universal distress signal for choking
- When the airway is severely obstructed, the patient’s skin may turn blue (cyanosis) and they may lose consciousness.
    - Cardiac arrest will follow if the obstruction is not quickly removed.
- There are several techniques that can be used to alleviate an airway obstruction.
    - The American Heart Association (AHA) recommends abdominal thrusts.
    - The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) recommends back blows or a combination of back blows and abdominal thrusts.
#### Heimlich Maneuver
1. Stand or kneel behind the patient and wrap your arms around their waist
2. Make a fist with one hand and place the thumb side against the abdomen, just above the umbilicus (navel) and well below the sternum (breastbone)
3. Grasp your fist with the other hand and give a quick, inward and upward thrust into the abdomen. The force of these thrusts will often be enough to relieve the obstruction
4. Continue delivering abdominal thrusts until the obstruction is relieved or the patient becomes unresponsive

#### Back Blows
1. Stand behind the person and slightly to one side. Reach across the person's chest by wrapping one arm either over the person's arm or under their armpit. Place the palm of that hand on the person's upper chest or shoulder. Leave your other hand free.
2. Have the person bend over at the waist to a 0-=degree angle (their upper body should be parallel to the ground or floor). Do not keep them in an upright or a vertical position. If they are not bending over when the jolt of the back blow dislodges the object, the dislodged object may become stuck deeper in the airway
3. With your fingers up, use the heel of your hand to firmly strike the person between their shoulder blades
4. If 5 back blows do not dislodge the object, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts

#### Chest Thrusts
>- In some circumstances, you may not be able to reach around the waist of the responsive patient because they are obese or in the later stages of pregnancy.
    - In these situations, you should perform chest thrusts.
1. Stand behind the patient with your arms under the patient’s armpits and wrap your arms around the chest.
2. Place the thumb side of one hand in the middle of the chest, between the nipples.
3. Grasp the fist with your other hand and pull inward on the chest until the obstruction is relieved or the patient becomes unresponsive.

When necessary, you can perform chest thrusts on a supine patient by kneeling close to them and delivering downward thrusts in the middle of the chest, between the nipples

#### Managing Airway Obstruction in Unresponsive Adults and Children
1. Carefully support the patient to the ground and immediately call for help (or send someone to call for help)
2. Perform chest compressions, utilzing the same landmark as you would for CPR. Perform 30 chest compressions if you are alone or if the patient is an adult; perform 15 compressions if ywo providers are present and the patient is a child. Note that for a choking patient who becomes unresponsive, a provider should begin chest compressions immediately after lowering the patient to the ground. Do not check for a pulse
3. Open the airway and look in the mouth. IF you see an object and can easily remove it, remove it with your fingers and attempt to ventilate. If you do not see an object, resume chest compressions.
4. Continue the sequence above until the obstruction is relieved or advanced life support personnel take over. After two minutes (about 5 cycles) of CPR, if someone has not already done so, the health care provider should go for help. Once the obstruction is relieved and your breaths produce visible chest rise, check for a pulse. If the patient does not have a pulse, continue CPR (compressions and ventilations) until help arrives
### Basic Life Support for Infants
#### Assessment
- Cardiac arrest in infants, as in children, usually results from:
    - Respiratory failure
    - Injury
    - Suffocation
    - Airway obstruction
    - Smoke inhalation
    - Infection
    - Drowning
    - [Sudden unexplained infant death (SUID)](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")
    - [Child abuse](https://elearning.jblearning.com/scormCourses/BLS_HCP_510478/Shell/main.html# "glossary")
- Begin by checking responsiveness.
- If the infant is responsive, assist if needed.
- If the infant is unresponsive, quickly look at the patient’s chest to see if it is rising and falling and check for a pulse.
- If the infant is unresponsive and is breathing normally, monitor their condition until help arrives.
- If the infant is not breathing or is breathing abnormally but has a pulse, provide rescue breathing.
- If both pulse and breathing are absent, begin CPR, starting with chest compressions.
- If there are no bystanders, provide 2 minutes of care and then use your cell phone, if available, to call 9-1-1 or activate the emergency response system.
#### Checking for Responsiveness, Breathing, and a Pulse
- When presented with a motionless infant, gently tap the infant and shout or flick the soles of the infant’s feet with your fingers.
- If the unresponsive infant is lying face down, turn them onto their back.
- If you are alone and without a cell phone, complete your assessment and provide 2 minutes of care before going for help.
- If the infant is unresponsive, assess for breathing by looking for the rising and falling of the chest while simultaneously checking for a pulse.
    - This should take no more than 10 seconds.
- Feel for a brachial pulse on the inside of the upper arm.
- Place your index and middle fingers on the inside of the arm and press gently to feel for a pulse.
    - Feel for at least 5 seconds but no more than 10 seconds.
- If a _definite_ pulse is present, provide rescue breathing.
- If the pulse is absent or if the pulse rate is less than 60 beats per minute with signs of poor perfusion (i.e., cyanosis, pallor, decreased level of consciousness), begin CPR starting with chest compressions.
#### Opening the Airway and Providing Rescue Breathing
- If an unresponsive infant is not breathing or is breathing abnormally but has a pulse, provide rescue breathing.
- Use the head tilt–chin lift maneuver unless you suspect a spinal injury.
- Place padding beneath the shoulders to facilitate opening the airway.
- The infant’s head should be tilted back less than a child’s.
    - Be careful not to hyperextend the head and neck.
- To lift the chin, place your finger(s) just under it, on the bony part of the jaw.
    - Do not press on the soft tissue under the infant’s chin.
- If you suspect a spinal injury, open the airway with the jaw-thrust maneuver.
    - If the jaw-thrust maneuver does not adequately open the airway, _carefully_ perform the head tilt–chin lift maneuver.
- After opening the infant’s airway, create a tight seal over the infant’s mouth and nose with a ventilation mask or other barrier device.
- As you gently blow into the infant’s mouth and nose, watch for the chest to rise and fall.
- Because infants normally breathe faster than adults do, provide rescue breathing at a rate of 20 to 30 breaths per minute (1 breath every 2 to 3 seconds).
- Do not overinflate the infant’s lungs by breathing too forcefully or too fast.
- Hyperventilation can result in:
    - Damage to the infant’s lungs
    - Vomiting
    - Distended abdomen, making it difficult for the lungs to fully inflate
    - Decreased amount of blood that returns to the heart
- Avoid these issues by maintaining an open airway and giving small puffs (1 second each)—just enough to produce visible chest rise.
- If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and attempt to deliver another breath.
- If two attempts at delivering rescue breathing are unsuccessful, suspect an airway obstruction that needs to be cleared.
#### CPR for Infants
>*CPR (chest compressions and rescue breaths) is indicated for an infant who is pulseless or whose heart rate is less than 60 beats per minute with signs of poor perfusion.*

1. Compressions are performed with the index and middle fingers of one hand, placed on the sternum one finger width below the nipple line.
2. With your fingers in the correct location, compress the chest with the pads of your fingertips.
3. Compress the chest at least one-third the anteroposterior depth of the chest (about 1.5 inches [4 cm]) at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
     Ensure that the chest fully recoils following each compression.
4. After 30 chest compressions, open the airway and deliver 2 rescue breaths.
5. If there are bystanders, have them call for help and retrieve an AED.
	If you are alone and without a cell phone, give 30 compressions and 2 breaths per cycle. Perform 2 minutes of CPR and then go for help.
6. If two health care providers are present, give 15 compressions and 2 breaths per cycle. Switch roles every 2 minutes to minimize fatigue.
7. Continue CPR unless:
	1. The infant begins to move.
	2. You are replaced by someone of equal or higher training.
	3. You are alone and become too fatigued to continue.
8. When the AED arrives, continue compressions while it is turned on and applied. Limit interruptions in chest compressions to 10 seconds or less.

>*When an advanced airway is in place during two-person infant CPR, ventilate the infant at a rate of 10 breaths per minute (1 breath every 6 seconds) and perform compressions at a rate of 100 to 120 per minute.
>Do not attempt to synchronize breaths and compressions.*

#### Managing the Responsive Infant With an Airway Obstruction
>*If the infant is coughing forcefully, has adequate air eschange, and has normal skin color, suspect a mild airway obstruction.
>Closely observe the infant, but do not interfere with attempts to expel the obstruction
>If the infant cannot cough, cry, or breath, is coughing weakly, is turning blue (cyanosis), or is making high-pitched sounds during inhalation (stridor), they have a severe airway obstruction and require immediate treatment.*

1. Position the infant facedown over your forearm with their head lower than the chest. Support the infants jaw with your hand
2. Lower the infant and your forearm to your thigh
3. Use the heel of your hand to give the infant five back blows between the shoulder blades. Deliver each back blow with enough force to dislodge the obstruction
4. Place the infant between your hands and arms and turn the infant face up
5. If the back blows did not dislodge the foreign body, give five chest compressions in the same location as you for the chest compressions during cardiac arrest
6. Observe the infant throughout the process to see whether the obstruction has been dislodged. If it has not, continue the cycles of five back blows and five chest compressions until the obstruction is dislodged or the infant becomes unresponsive

#### Managing the Responsive Infant With an Airway Obstruction
1. Position the infant on a firm, flat surface and immediately call for help (or send someone to call for help)
2. Perform chest compressions, using the same landmark as you would for CPR. Perform 30 chest compressions if you are alone, perform 15 compressions if 2 providers are present. As with a child or an adult, do not check for a pulse before beginning chest compressions on a choking infant who becomes unresponsive
3. Open the airway and look in the mouth. If you see an object and can easily remove it, try to remove it with your finger, and attempt to ventilate, If you do not see an object, resume chest compressions
4. Continue the above sequence until the obstruction is relieved or advanced life support personnel take over

>After 2 minutes (about 5 cycles) of CPR, if someone has not already done so, the health care provider should go for help

>Once the obstruction is relieved and your breaths produce visible chest rise, check for a pulse. If the patient has a pulse but is not breathing or is breathing abnormally (i.e. only gasping), continue rescue breathing. If the patient does not have a pulse (or a pulse less than 60 beats per minute with signs of poor perfusion, continue CPR until the help arrives
