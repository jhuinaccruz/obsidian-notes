This “constant substance” refers to a firm internal commitment to virtue, ritual (li), righteousness (yi), and cultivated nature.*
>
>*The idea is that, just as Heaven and Earth have unchanging patterns (like the seasons, the sun and moon rising and setting, etc.), the gentleman too has an unchanging core — he does not allow his emotions, desires, or external pressures to cause him to act in ways that violate his cultivated moral sense. While the gentleman stands firm on principle, the petty person is blown about by circumstance, acting without regard for a larger moral order.*
>
>*In short:*
>- *Gentleman (junzi): Has a stable, cultivated moral character; consistent and principled.*
>- *Petty person (xiaoren): Is guided by base desires; inconsistent and self-serving.*

In a letter to Arnold Ruge, Marx advocates a position according to which “we do not anticipate the world dogmatically but wish to discover the new world by criticism of the old.” What is Marx saying in this passage about the nature of “criticism”?  What is the key assumption or principle behind Marx’s favored approach?
>[!quote]
>According to ChatGPT:
>*In that letter (to Arnold Ruge, September 1843), Marx insists that genuine “criticism” is not the imposition of preconceived (“dogmatic”) ideas onto reality, but rather an active, practical interrogation of existing social, political, intellectual—and even personal—arrangements in order to unearth the conditions and dynamics out of which a genuinely new world can emerge. Three interlinked points follow:*
>1. *Criticism as practice, not idle judgment*
>	*For Marx, to “criticize” is not simply to point out flaws in abstract propositions or to engage in theoretical disputation. Rather it is a “practical” activity: it intervenes in existing relations, exposes their internal contradictions, and thereby creates the conditions for transformation. In his own words, criticism “sets out from real, existing society and brings into relief those contradictions which are the motive force of history.”*
>2. *Discovery through negation, not deduction from first principles*
>	*A dogmatic philosophy would begin with fixed, a priori categories—“human nature,” “reason,” “freedom,” etc.—and then deduce what society must be. Marx rejects that. Instead, he wants to “discover the new world by criticism of the old”—that is, to let the real movement of history, with its class struggles and material transformations, reveal both the limits of the old order and the shape of what is to come.*
>
>*Key assumption: reality is dynamic and contradictory*
>
>*The principle behind Marx’s approach is that social reality is neither harmonious nor fully intelligible in static categories. It is riven by contradictions (e.g. between use-value and exchange-value, labor and capital) that can only be grasped by a concrete-historical method. Only by exposing those contradictions in practice—turning “must” into “can,” as he later puts it—can one begin to shape a new, non-alienated order.*
>
>*In sum: Marx’s “criticism” is a form of active, historically grounded inquiry that proceeds by exposing and engaging the contradictions of existing social forms rather than by imposing abstract dogmas. Its foundational principle is that the material and social world is internally dynamic and contradictory—and that only by uncovering those contradictions in practice can genuine change be understood and brought about.*
