## Hedonic Disregulation Theory of Addiction

> Addiction results from three stages of "hedonistic disregulation"

1. __Binge/Intoxication__: the individual consumes a substance for its rewarding and pleasurable effects
	the [[Ventral striatum]] and the [[Basal Ganglia]] are involved in this process
2. __Withdrawal/Negative Symptom(s)__: the individual experiences a negative emotional state due to the absence of the drug(s)
	the [[Amygdala]] and the Bed Nucleus of the Stria Terminalis
3. __Preoccupation/Anticipation__: the individual seeks the substance after a period of abstinence
	the [[Prefrontal Cortex]] and the [[Orbital Frontal Cortex]] are involved in this

>With repeated use of a drug, brain reward systems become dysregulated, and their level of activity does not return to normal (loss of drug reward + sensitization of negative mood state = shift in initial mood set point)
>
>Over time, new (more negative) mood set points are established, and a person must take larger and larger doses of the drug to achieve the same degree of pleasure/euphoria (due to tolerance
>
>At the same time, reinforcing value for other non-drug rewards (e.g. social or recreational activities) are diminished because they no longer stimulate the reward system sufficiently, and participation in non-drug activities is thus reduced, leaving more time for drug taking

### Maladaptive Neuroadaptations

Occur in areas of the brain associated in the role of addiction development
- __[[Prefrontal Cortex]]__
	>hypoactivity here can negatively affect inhibitory control, decisionmaking, and planning; misuse denial
- __[[Nucleus Accumbens]]__ and the __[[Ventral Tegmental Area]]__
	>saliency/motivation for the drug (“wanting”) is sensitized through the actions of dopamine in this system, though euphoria is reduced (due to PD tolerance)
- __[[Orbital Frontal Cortex]]__ and __[[Basal Ganglia]]__
	>motivation/drive for non-drug reinforcers are blunted; drug motor habit formation occurs
- __[[Amygdala]]___ and __[[Hippocampus]]__
	>memory & emotional conditioning negatively affected, producing an overall negative/dysphoric emotional state (“anti-reward”)
	
>PFC sends massive glutamatergic projections to NAc, BG, amygdala and hippocampus; druginduced hypoactivity in PFC can lead to dysfunction of these circuits, causing intrusive thinking, powerful drug craving, denial of misuse and impulsivity characteristic of addiction

| Risk Factors                     | Protective Factors              |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Aggressive behavior in childhood | Good Self-control               |
| Lack of Parental Supervision     | Parental Monitoring and Support |
| Poor Social Skills               | Positive Relationships          |
| Drug Experimentation             | Good Grades                     |
| Availability of drugs at school  | School anti-drug policies       |
| Community poverty                | Neighborhood Resources          |
>With continued abstinence, Ventral Striatum dopamine receptor expression eventually normalizes, but not all brain changes that occur during addiction return to normal

# Alcohol I - History, Fermentation & Distillation

Drinkable alcohol is a product of the fermentation of sugar
- Drinkable alcohol is also ethanol
		Other common alcohols are wood alcohol (methanol) and isopropanol (rubbing alcohol)
## Fermentation

In order to produce fermentation, one needs
- __A sugar source__
- __Anaerobic conditions__ (low oxygen)
- __Yeast__ (Sacchoromyces/Brettanomyces)

This process produces Ethanol and Carbon Dioxide
	Ethanol concentrations only reach about 10-15% (otherwise the yeast is destroyed)

## History

__around 8000 BCE__ - Fermentation probably began with the development of agriculture from the Neolithic peoples

Early alcoholic beverages began with:
- __beer__ from grain(s)
- __wine__ from fruit
- __mead__ from honey
- __さけ__ from fermenting rice

>To obtain stronger alcoholic beverages, the fermented liquid is boiled to vaporize the alcohol, which is then condensed back to a liquid by cooling, allowing for alcohol concentrations of up to 95%

__Later on in the Middle Ages__ - brandy became the first distilled spirit

Distillation made gin became popular in Europe and whiskey in the United States

__1785__ - Rush: *An Inquiry into the Effects of Ardent Spirits*

__Late 1700s__ - concern about the consequences of alcohol consumption led to the "temperance" movement in the United States

__Today__ - During the COVID-19 pandemic, liquor stores were considered "essential businesses"
