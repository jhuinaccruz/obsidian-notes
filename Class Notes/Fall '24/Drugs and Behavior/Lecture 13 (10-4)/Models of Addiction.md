## Moral Theory
*Addiction is the result of moral weakness*
- historically held for most of human history, still held today
### Challenges
- first challenged by the "temperance movement"
- "inebriety is a disease" - Benjamin Rush (of the American Association for the Cure of Inebriates)
	- Not based on neuroscience, but rather a *humanitarian* perspective
- resurfaced in 1935 with the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous in 1935
	- "one drink = one drunk"

## Dependence Model
*Drug withdrawal is so painful and distressing that individuals are willing to sacrifice almost anything to avoid it*
- used to explain addiction to drugs that cause severe withdrawal effects
### Challenges
- some highly addictive drugs produce less severe withdrawal
- some drugs produce withdrawal effects but are not addictive


## Positive Reinforcement Model
*The drug has such a desirable effect on the individual that it reinforces its own consumption and that leads to addiction*
- animals were seen as. immune to addiction; addiction was seen as a human train linked to free will
- Lab animals are not intelligent enough to make the association between the drug and its pleasurable
### Challenges
- Tolerance to the high can become substantial, which in theory *reduces* drug reinforcement
- Drug misuse often occurs even when reinforcers produce *negative* effects
- Operant conditioning (with intravenous self-administrating methods) experiments have disproven the idea that animals cannot get addicted

## Disease Model
*Physiological changes caused by drugs leads to addiction*
- The most widely-accepted model for addiction
- There is a lot of evidence for long-term neuroplastic (structural) changes that occur in the brain as a result of substance addiction
	- lead to the disregulation of important brain circuits
- the United States took the position of addiction being a disease (Disease Model of Drug Abuse) (__DMDA__)
### Challenges
- a disease models overlooks the social factors
- takes away personal agency
- should be instead called a "disorder" (such as in the DSM-5)
