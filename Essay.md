## The Story

Before Argus becomes a guardian of Io, they both have backgrounds that, while limited and simple, provide a narrative where they exist to a degree of normality. Argus Panoptes, for example, is credited with heroic feats such as killing the Arcadian Bull and even Echidna (Apollodorus, citation needed).

Etymologically, his name is largely acknowledged as meaning “The All-Seeing from Argos” (Apollodorus, Oxford Classical, citation needed), while the understanding of where and how his eyes exist changes from writer to writer. The earliest mention of Argus, in an excerpt in the Aegimius, reveals that he is a being with four eyes that “look every way” (Schol. annotation, Aegimius, citation needed). Later interpretations will acknowledge anything from a hundred eyes (Ovid, Metamorphoses, citation needed) to eyes all over his body (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca, citation needed), In art especially, early on he is portrayed as a man with eyes covering his body, exemplified on pieces such as a clay water jug at Boston’s Museum of Fine Arts, depicting Zeus and Hera observing Hermes fighting Argus (art citation needed?).

Recountings of Io largely emphasize her birth as a daughter of Inachus the king (Herodotus, Histories, citation needed) or the river god (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca, citation needed). As a nymph or as a princess, she becomes a priestess of Hera (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca, citation needed). [add thing about key-bearer]

The most famous telling of their story occurs in the Metamorphoses, where Ovid details how Jove (Jupiter/Zeus) forces himself onto Io in a cloud of smoke to stop her running away. As Juno (Hera) notices this supernatural cloud of smoke and decides to investigate such an unnatural occurrence, Jupiter decides to turn Io into a white heifer. Juno on arrival demands this cow, (perhaps a bit sarcastically) deeming it to be too beautiful to sacrifice, to which he reluctantly agrees. Juno then hands Io the cow over to Argus, who assumes cowherd responsibilities over her, letting her feed by day and otherwise keeping her tethered up at night. Io, unable to converse with anybody due to her condition, one day wanders to a stream by where she used to frequent where she finds her father. Unrecognizable to him, she instead traces her name into the dirt, by which he then cries that he has found her in such a state. In his despair, Inachus gets the help of one god Mercury to free her from her captor. He does this by at first playing a soothing melody on his reeds, enticing Argus’ interest. Argus and Mercury then strike a conversation, passing the hours whilst only some of his eyes slept. Sensing weakness, with the help of a partial retelling of Pan and Syrinx’ story, combined with his magic wand, put Argus and his eyes completely to sleep and cut his head off, earning him of his most famous nicknames in Argeiphontes, or Argus killer (citations needed). An enraged Juno then sends a Fury that chases Io into the Nile in Egypt, by which then Jupiter swears that he will no longer pursue Io. Juno allows Io to change back into her nymph form, in time for her to give birth to a son of Zeus in Epaphus.

Perhaps the most famous appearance of Io in drama is in Aeschylus’ Prometheus Bound; although largely focusing on its obvious protagonist Prometheus the Titan, the same Titan that defied Zeus and gave fire to humanity. A three-part trilogy, Bound takes place while Prometheus is bound with his liver being consumed by the Caucasus Eagle, where he is encountered by Io. Unique to Aeschylus’ tellings, Zeus attempted to seduce Io through dreams, and through initial resistance, he instead turned his coercion into manipulation through her father, King Inachus. In his search for answers, he turns to the oracle at Dodona, who relays Zeus’ power and will to strike them with a bolt of lightning down unless he ousts his daughter from his kingdom, dooming her to wander the Earth. Inachus complies, and as she is kicked out of her father’s, her transformation into a more bovine creature by growing horns, as well as becoming chased by the infamous gadfly.

## Interpretations

Sources as early as Herodotus’ Histories write of potential rationalizations from the Persians and Phoenicians at the time, one where Io, daughter of Inachus, is a princess of Argos, kidnapped by the Phoenicians, and another where Io willingly stays with a Phoenician out of fear of shaming and detection (Herodotus, Histories, citation needed 1.1-5?)

Malalas’ Chronography details both rationalizations for Io and Argus as humans respectively. 

Io is described as a daughter of an Argivean King Inachus and wife Melia. A king from the west (either Zeus or Picus) then kidnaps her and rapes her, leading to her giving birth to daughter Libye. Her shame of the situation then leads her to sail away to Egypt, then later possibly to Syria. In the case where she did make it to Syria, however, he recounts that Inachus sent her brothers and company to look for her, eventually finding themselves in Syria, long after she died. She then appears to them in a dream and announces herself as a heifer. This prophetic dream they had made them begin a city after her namesake, transforming Mount Sipion where they had the dream into the city of Iopolis, along with a sanctuary for her. Instances of conflation between Arguses occur on the account of his lineage as a son of Arestor, or Arestorides. Specifically, an instance of slippage between Argus Panoptes, son of Arestor, as well as Argus the Argonaut (for Argus Panoptes: Apollodorus’ Bibliotheca, Ovid’s Metamorphoses; for Argus the Argonaut: Apollodorus’ Argonautica). This association can be seen in Malalas’ Chronography, who rationalizes Argus as looked at “by all sides”, hence the All-Seeing, and as one who discovered craftsmanship.

More conceptual and contextual readings of this story, such as the one of Rachel Gottsman which builds on her doctoral research, see Io’s journey through the Mediterranean as a mapping and conceptualization of different parts of modern-day Europe and Africa; more specifically, the unification of Greek self-conceptions with the more distant and abstract ideas of other lands. She proposes a useful model: the Maritime Mediation Model, where the Mediterranean is a bridge between different parts of the world.

Perhaps the most useful conclusion from mapping said model onto ancient times is one where stories such as that of Io were useful to the Greek because they solidified far, distant, and otherwise abstract locations into real objects into the collective conscience. Similar to the way one memorizes directions of north and south, east and west, through clever mnemonics and such, Io’s own journey, among many other stories, can be seen as memorizing as well as contextualizing tools for the world around them.

Relating back to Io’s own journey in the context of the gods, along with her genealogy revealing even more travelers and founders, shows that parts of Ancient Greece aren’t simply cities or civilizations, but places founded as a result of divine intervention. Her lineage can therefore be seen as a bridge connecting the supernatural world of the Titans and gods to that of humans.

A relationship less explored in antiquity, though equally important, is perhaps the one between Argus and Juno, explored at length in Patricia Salzman-Mitchell’s study titled “A Web of Fantasies: Gaze, Image, and Gender in Ovid's Metamorphoses”. Perhaps fittingly, her primary reading is Ovid’s literary masterpiece through a feminist understanding of the male gaze. (Laura Mulvey, further reading + citation needed) This idea of gaze is central to Argus, who called the All-Seeing for a reason, who is related to Juno, Salzman-Mitchell argues, as a proxy for her own vigilance and gaze of Io. She goes on to talk about Argus’ own panoptic view as a way of control over an already immobilized and disagented Io. This kind of narrative, one where a female subject’s agency is in tension with another figure trying to control them, is not unique to Io alone; Salzman-Mitchell points to other stories in the Metamorphoses carrying the same occurrences. (Chapter 2, citation needed)

Gaze isn’t limited to simply Argus and Io through Hera, but also Juno’s own desire to reclaim Jupiter’s gaze for her own, devoid of any distractions such as that of Io. As such, Io was a threat to Juno’s wants, and was punished as evidenced by her journey through the Mediterranean. (citation needed)
